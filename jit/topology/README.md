# JIT Topology

**Status:** concept captured; no implementation authorized yet.

**Workspace:** `bonoj/bonoj.github.io` → `jit/topology/`

## Origin — 2026-10-25

### Observation (user)

> What I'm noticing is that the tree is a first class primitive for you to compose and animate delightfully with very little effort.

The observation concerns **the model-mediated creative/engineering process**, not merely botanical simulation: branching trees appear unusually cheap for the model to compose and animate convincingly, compared with coherent human figures, interiors, or architectural worlds. This suggests investigating an atlas of native expressive primitives and composing systems from forms that are naturally reliable and inexpensive to generate.

### Proposal (user)

> What about about using the trees growing topology to push fields and flows and particle anchor points?

### Response — tree as computational substrate (assistant)

**The tree stops being geometry and becomes a computational substrate.** Instead of growing a tree *inside* a simulation, grow a tree that **organizes the simulation itself**.

A single evolving graph supports three coupled systems:

1. **Fields generated by the tree.** Every branch contributes to a spatial field. Its influence can depend on thickness, orientation, age, temperature, charge, or other properties. Nearby particles respond to the combined field. As the tree grows, the field changes continuously; branches can attract, repel, bend trajectories, or induce vortices.
2. **Flows transported through the tree.** Edges act as conduits; nodes as junctions. Water, electricity, nutrients, heat, signals, or abstract conserved quantities travel through branches. At junctions, incoming flow is distributed, stored, consumed, or returned. Branch thickness can adapt to throughput.
3. **Particles anchored to topology.** Particles need not have fixed world coordinates. They can attach to a branch by stable edge identity and parameter, e.g. `{ edge: 42, t: 0.73 }`. As a branch bends, grows, or sways, its anchors move with it. Particles may slide, accumulate at junctions, detach, or seed growth.

### Feedback loop

**Topology grows → fields and flows change → particles respond → local conditions alter growth → topology grows.**

New nodes and edges alter transport and influence. Particles move, attach, accumulate, and detach. Local conditions can cause branches to extend, thicken, bend, split, or die back.

The key engineering requirement is **continuity through topology changes**: when an edge subdivides or a branch splits, preserve anchored particles and transported quantities. Stable edge identities, explicit junctions, and conservative remapping prevent the rendering from appearing continuous while the simulation silently loses its history.

### Generalization

The same graph machinery can produce branching organisms, roots, vascular systems, lightning, fungal colonies, river networks, electrical circuits, coral, or procedural nervous systems by changing growth and transport rules.

A particularly promising experiment is **a tree that grows toward particles attracted by its own field**, while those particles influence where new branches emerge. No predetermined silhouette or scripted animation: a compact generative feedback system.

**Working thesis:** topology, dynamics, and rendering can share the same underlying structure. The tree does not merely depict a phenomenon; **the tree computes one**.
